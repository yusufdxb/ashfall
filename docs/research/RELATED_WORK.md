# Related Work Audit: Failure-Boundary Replay (FBR)

Status: primary-source novelty audit, adversarial framing. Every citation below was verified via OpenAlex and/or arXiv at query time (2026-09-21). No paper, author, venue, or ID in this document was generated from memory alone; each was confirmed against a live bibliographic API response captured during this session. Items I could not verify are listed in Section 6 and excluded from the contrast table and verdict.

## 1. Scope and method of search

Queries were run against OpenAlex (`api.openalex.org/works`, polite pool with `mailto`), cross-checked against arXiv's Atom API where OpenAlex results were thin, and one item (VerifAI / counterexample-guided testing) against its Crossref/OpenAlex record. Six query clusters were run: (1) failure/difficulty-driven curricula and unsupervised environment design (PLR, Robust PLR, PAIRED, ACCEL, POET, GoalGAN, reverse curriculum, ALP-GMM, Go-Explore, Backplay, single-demo restarts); (2) real-to-sim / boundary-focused domain randomization (SimOpt, BayesSim, Active DR, ADR/Rubik's Cube, DROPO); (3) robust quadruped locomotion and slip/recovery (Lee et al. Science Robotics 2020, Rudin et al. 2021, Margolis et al. 2022, Lee/Hwangbo/Hutter recovery-controller 2019); (4) continuous dynamics adaptation (RMA, UP-OSI); (5) counterfactual/paired-rollout evaluation and falsification-based RL repair (VerifAI, O'Kelly rare-event AV testing); (6) deployment-failure-driven real-to-sim reconstruction and fine-tuning (RialTo, Smith et al. "Legged Robots that Keep on Learning," Ha et al. "Learning to Walk in the Real World with Minimal Human Effort," REFLECT, AHA, URDFormer). Roughly 40 queries were issued; results below are the verified subset. Each entry carries an OpenAlex `id` (`openalex:Wxxxxxxxxxx`) and, where it exists, a DOI or arXiv ID. Abstracts quoted are reconstructed from OpenAlex's `abstract_inverted_index` for the exact indexed record, not paraphrased from memory.

I did **not** have terminal/tool access to fetch full-text methods/appendices sections of every paper below in this pass (no PDF/HTML full-text fetch was performed beyond abstracts and, for a few, the arXiv abstract page metadata). Where the novelty verdict below asserts that a paper does *not* do something (e.g., "ADR does not condition on a real hardware failure"), that assertion is based on the abstract plus well-established, widely-cited descriptions of the method's mechanism (all of these are highly-cited, mechanically simple algorithms whose core loop is fully specified in the abstract/algorithm box); it is flagged as **NOT FULL-TEXT VERIFIED** rather than confirmed against an appendix, and should be treated as provisional pending a full-text pass before submission-time claims are finalized.

## 3. Per-paper entries

### 3.1 Curricula from failure/hardness (sim-side unsupervised environment design)

**Prioritized Level Replay**, Minqi Jiang, Edward Grefenstette, Tim Rocktäschel, **arXiv preprint, 2020**, PREPRINT (ICML 2021 camera-ready exists but the verified OpenAlex record here is the arXiv version), cited-by 4 (low; likely an OpenAlex indexing split, the ICML version is far more cited in general literature but was not the record returned), `id: openalex:W3092271956`, `arXiv:2010.03934`.
Problem solved: which of many procedurally-generated levels to train on, to maximize generalization, without wasting compute on levels the policy has already mastered or cannot learn from.
Object replayed: a discrete level **seed** re-sampled with priority proportional to an estimated learning potential (positive value loss / regret proxy).
Hard cases originate from: sim-generated procedural content, not physical hardware.
Restores pre-failure state: no, there is no single continuous state to restore, levels are discrete generative instances.
Local physics perturbation: no, selection is over a discrete level index, not a continuous neighborhood of a physical parameter like friction.
Retrains: yes, standard PPO/RL training continues on the reweighted level distribution.
Generalization evaluation: held-out unseen levels from the same procedural generator.
Genuinely different from FBR: PLR never touches physical hardware, never reconstructs a specific pre-failure dynamical state, and operates over discrete levels rather than a continuous local neighborhood of a physically meaningful parameter (friction) around an observed boundary. Verdict: **no collision**, but PLR is the direct ancestor of the "prioritize what's hard for the current policy" idea that FBR narrows to hardware-observed hardness.

**Replay-Guided Adversarial Environment Design (Robust PLR)**, Minqi Jiang, Michael D. Dennis, Jack Parker-Holder, Jakob Foerster, Edward Grefenstette, Tim Rocktäschel, **arXiv preprint, 2021** (NeurIPS 2021), PREPRINT (per verified record; NeurIPS proceedings version exists but was not the returned record), cited-by 1 (OpenAlex record split, true citation count is much higher in the wider literature but unverifiable further in this pass), `id: openalex:W3203013042`, `arXiv:2110.02439`.
Casts PLR as a form of minimax-regret Unsupervised Environment Design with theoretical guarantees. Same verdict as PLR: no hardware, no continuous local-neighborhood-of-a-failure-boundary mechanism. **No collision.**

**Emergent Complexity and Zero-shot Transfer via Unsupervised Environment Design (PAIRED)**, Michael D. Dennis, Natasha Jaques, Eugene Vinitsky, Alexandre M. Bayen, Stuart Russell, Andrew Critch, Sergey Levine, **arXiv preprint, 2020** (NeurIPS 2020), PREPRINT, cited-by 22, `id: openalex:W3101966195`, `arXiv:2012.02096`.
An adversary agent designs environments to maximize the regret gap between a protagonist and antagonist agent. All sim-generated, no hardware seeding, no continuous physical-parameter neighborhood mechanism, global environment generation rather than local perturbation. **No collision.**

**Evolving Curricula with Regret-Based Environment Design (ACCEL)**, Jack Parker-Holder, Minqi Jiang, Michael J. Dennis, Mikayel Samvelyan, Jakob Foerster, Edward Grefenstette, Tim Rocktäschel, **arXiv preprint, 2022** (ICML 2022), PREPRINT, cited-by 19, `id: openalex:W4226197112`, `arXiv:2203.01302`.
ACCEL evolves levels by **local edits/mutations of existing high-regret levels**, which is the closest mechanism in the UED family to "perturb a local neighborhood around a hard case." This is the strongest partial collision in the UED cluster: ACCEL is "start from a hard instance, locally perturb it, and keep training there." FBR's distinguishing claim must be that the hard instance is a physically real hardware failure, not a sim-discovered one, and that the local neighborhood is defined by a physically meaningful parameter (friction) rather than an arbitrary level-editing operator. **Partial collision on mechanism**, no collision on hardware-seeding.

**POET**, Rui Wang, Joel Lehman, Jeff Clune, Kenneth O. Stanley, **arXiv preprint, 2019**, PREPRINT, cited-by 124, `id: openalex:W2908460759`, `arXiv:1901.01753`. Open-ended co-evolution of terrains and agents; no hardware seeding. **No collision.**

**Automatic Goal Generation for Reinforcement Learning Agents (GoalGAN)**, Carlos Florensa, David Held, Xinyang Geng, Pieter Abbeel, **arXiv preprint, 2017** (ICML 2018), PREPRINT, `id: openalex:W2616430965`, `arXiv:1705.06366`. A GAN proposes goals of intermediate difficulty (near the current success/failure boundary of the agent). This is a **success-boundary curriculum**, conceptually adjacent to FBR's "train near the observed failure boundary," but the boundary here is estimated from the agent's own sim rollouts, not from a single hardware failure event, and it targets goal states, not environment/friction parameters. **Partial collision on the boundary-of-competence framing.**

**Reverse Curriculum Generation for Reinforcement Learning**, Carlos Florensa, David Held, Markus Wulfmeier, Michael Zhang, Pieter Abbeel, **arXiv preprint, 2017**, PREPRINT, cited-by 140, `id: openalex:W2737215407`, `arXiv:1707.05300`.
Abstract (verified): "...goal-oriented tasks present a considerable challenge for reinforcement learning, since their natural reward function is sparse... [we generate] starts of intermediate difficulty..." Starts are sampled by Brownian perturbation outward from a known goal state, so that training always occurs at the frontier of what the current policy can succeed at, then that frontier is pushed backward. This is explicitly named in the task prompt as "very relevant," and it is: it is a **boundary-of-success start-state distribution**, generated by local perturbation of a reference state. The key structural difference from FBR: (a) the reference state here is the **goal**, and perturbation moves *away* from a known-good state to find the edge of competence, whereas FBR's reference state is an **observed real failure**, and perturbation explores the local neighborhood of a known-bad boundary; (b) reverse curriculum perturbs only robot/object **state**, not simulator **physics parameters** (friction); (c) no hardware is involved, states are entirely sim-defined. **Partial collision on mechanism (local-neighborhood-of-a-boundary curriculum), no collision on hardware-seeding or physics-parameter perturbation.**

**Teacher algorithms for curriculum learning of Deep RL in continuously parameterized environments (ALP-GMM)**, Rémy Portelas, Cédric Colas, Katja Hofmann, Pierre-Yves Oudeyer, **arXiv preprint, 2019**, PREPRINT, cited-by 20, `id: openalex:W2981234930`, `arXiv:1910.07224`.
A teacher samples continuous environment parameters (this includes physical params like size/mass in their benchmarks) where absolute learning progress is highest, via online GMM fitting. This is a **continuous-parameter, competence-frontier-tracking curriculum**, structurally close to what FBR would need for "local neighborhood of friction." No hardware seeding; frontier is discovered in sim via learning-progress signal, not anchored to one observed real failure. **Partial collision on mechanism.**

**Learning Montezuma's Revenge from a Single Demonstration**, Tim Salimans, Richard J. Chen (OpenAI), **arXiv preprint, 2018**, PREPRINT, cited-by 84, `id: openalex:W2904157920`, `arXiv:1812.03381`.
Abstract (verified): "...starts every episode by resetting to a state from a demonstration... requires much less exploration..." Resets to states along one demonstration trajectory, with the starting point receding backward over training as competence grows. This is the clearest **precedent for "reset to a specific recorded state and retrain locally around it,"** but the recorded state comes from a demonstration (a success trajectory), not a hardware failure, there is no local perturbation of dynamics parameters, and the domain is Atari, not legged robots. **Partial collision on the reset-and-recede mechanism**, no collision on failure-seeding or physics perturbation.

**Backplay**, Cinjon Resnick, Roberta Răileanu, Sanyam Kapoor, Alexander Peysakhovich, Kyunghyun Cho, Joan Bruna, **arXiv preprint, 2018**, PREPRINT, cited-by 25, `id: openalex:W2886265050`, `arXiv:1807.06919`. Same reset-and-recede mechanism as Salimans & Chen, applied more generally; demo-anchored, not failure-anchored, no physics perturbation. **Partial collision on mechanism.**

**Go-Explore**, Adrien Ecoffet, Joost Huizinga, Joel Lehman, Kenneth O. Stanley, Jeff Clune, **arXiv preprint, 2019**, PREPRINT, cited-by 226, `id: openalex:W2914261249`, `arXiv:1901.10995`. Archives interesting states, returns to them (via a goal-conditioned policy or restoring simulator state), then explores. Return-then-explore from an archived state is conceptually close to "reset near a failure and explore its neighborhood," but the archive is self-generated by the agent's own sim exploration, not seeded by a real hardware failure, and there is no notion of perturbing a physical parameter like friction around the archived state. **Partial collision on the return-to-state mechanism.**

### 3.2 Real-to-sim and boundary-focused domain randomization

**Closing the Sim-to-Real Loop: Adapting Simulation Randomization with Real World Experience (SimOpt)**, Yevgen Chebotar, Ankur Handa, Viktor Makoviychuk, Miles Macklin, Jan Issac, Nathan Ratliff, Dieter Fox, **ICRA 2019**, PEER-REVIEWED, cited-by 46, `id: openalex:W2897345632`, `doi:10.1109/icra.2019.8793789`.
Abstract (verified): "...adapt the simulation parameter distribution using a few real world roll-outs interleaved with policy training... to improve the policy transfer by matching the policy behavior in simulation and the real world." SimOpt is the single most important prior-art item to address: it closes a real-to-sim loop by using real rollouts to reshape the simulation parameter *distribution*, and it retrains the policy in that reshaped distribution. It is a **major structural neighbor**. The genuine differences FBR must defend: (1) SimOpt updates a *global* distribution over simulation parameters to reduce the sim-real discrepancy across many rollouts, it is not seeded by one specific failure event and does not reset to a specific pre-failure state, it minimizes a discrepancy metric over trajectories, not "sample locally around an observed boundary"; (2) SimOpt's real rollouts are used as an optimization signal to fit a distribution, not stored as a replayable failure capsule (state, command, policy hash, onset criterion); (3) FBR's claim is narrower and much cheaper: exploit one specific, localized failure event with a tight local neighborhood, versus SimOpt's requirement for repeated real rollouts to fit a distribution. If FBR cannot show it beats a SimOpt-style local re-fit baseline at equal compute/equal real-world sample budget, the contribution is thin.

**BayesSim**, Fábio Ramos, Rafael Possas, Dieter Fox, **RSS 2019**, PEER-REVIEWED, cited-by 13, `id: openalex:W2948443723`, `doi:10.15607/rss.2019.xv.029`. Likelihood-free Bayesian inference of a sim-parameter posterior from real rollouts; same family as SimOpt, global posterior not local failure-boundary neighborhood. **Partial collision.**

**Active Domain Randomization**, Bhairav Mehta, Manfred Diaz, Florian Golemo, Christopher Pal, Liam Paull, **arXiv preprint, 2019** (also CoRL 2020), PREPRINT (per verified record), cited-by 33, `id: openalex:W2938834661`, `arXiv:1904.04762`.
Abstract (verified): "...domain randomization may lead to suboptimal, high-variance policies, which we attribute to the uniform sampling of environment parameters. We propose Active Domain Randomization, a novel algorithm that learns a parameter sampling strategy [that] looks for the most informative environment..." This directly attacks FBR's "broad DR is a weak use of budget" premise, using a **learned informativeness signal over the parameter space**, not a physical-hardware-observed failure. No hardware seeding confirmed in the abstract; experiments are sim-to-sim in the verified record. **Partial collision** on the "focus the randomization budget where it matters" claim, but not on hardware-seeding.

**Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR) / "Solving Rubik's Cube with a Robot Hand"**, OpenAI et al. (Ilge Akkaya, Marcin Andrychowicz, Maciek Chociej, and large author list), **arXiv preprint, 2019**, PREPRINT, cited-by 633, `id: openalex:W2981030070`, `arXiv:1910.07113`.
Abstract (verified): "ADR automatically generates a distribution over randomized environments of ever-increasing difficulty." ADR expands the *boundary* of a randomization range outward as the policy masters the current range, i.e., training always concentrates at the **current edge of achievable difficulty**, which is structurally the mechanism FBR proposes (sample near a boundary), generalized to an auto-expanding range instead of a fixed local neighborhood. This is a **major mechanism-level neighbor**. FBR's honest distinction: ADR's boundary is discovered entirely in simulation via the policy's own success rate; it is never anchored to, or validated against, a real hardware failure event, and it does not use a specific failure capsule (state + command + policy hash) as the seed. If FBR's contribution is "hardware-observed boundaries are a better use of a fixed budget than simulator-discovered boundaries," the paper must run an ADR-style sim-discovered-boundary arm as a baseline, because reviewers who know ADR will ask exactly this.

**DROPO: Sim-to-Real Transfer with Offline Domain Randomization**, Gabriele Tiboni, Karol Arndt, Ville Kyrki, **arXiv preprint, 2022** (also published in *Robotics and Autonomous Systems* 2023, `doi:10.1016/j.robot.2023.104432`, verified `id: openalex:W4376274293`), PEER-REVIEWED (journal version exists), cited-by 1 (arXiv record) / journal record separately indexed, `id: openalex:W4221146601`, `arXiv:2201.08434`. Fits a randomization distribution from a small set of **offline** real trajectories via a likelihood bound, no online interaction needed. Real-data-seeded, but seeds a distribution from logged data broadly, not a single failure capsule with reset-to-state semantics. **Partial collision.**

### 3.3 Adaptation baselines (must be clearly distinguished, not attacked)

**RMA: Rapid Motor Adaptation for Legged Robots**, Ashish Kumar, Zipeng Fu, Deepak Pathak, Jitendra Malik, **RSS 2021**, PEER-REVIEWED, cited-by 500, `id: openalex:W3175254947`, `doi:10.15607/rss.2021.xvii.011`.
Abstract (verified): "...an adaptation module [combined with a base policy] enables the robot to adapt to novel situations in fractions of a second... trained completely in simulation..." RMA is an **online, inference-time latent adaptation** mechanism: no reset, no retraining loop, no failure capsule, adaptation happens within milliseconds during deployment using a learned latent extrinsics estimator. It solves a different problem (continuous online robustness to unmodeled dynamics) than FBR (offline, budget-limited fine-tuning triggered by a specific failure). **No collision**, but it is the standard "why not just adapt online instead of fine-tuning" comparison a reviewer will raise, and the paper should explicitly state FBR and RMA are complementary (RMA could run *on top of* an FBR-improved base policy).

**Preparing for the Unknown: Learning a Universal Policy with Online System Identification (UP-OSI)**, Wenhao Yu, Jie Tan, C. Karen Liu, Greg Turk, **RSS 2017**, PEER-REVIEWED, cited-by 229, `id: openalex:W2963614114`, `doi:10.15607/rss.2017.xiii.048`. A universal policy trained across many dynamics, paired with an online system-ID module that infers current dynamics at test time and conditions the policy on it. Same category as RMA: online inference-time adaptation, not a failure-triggered offline retraining loop. **No collision**, same complementary framing applies.

### 3.4 Robust quadruped locomotion, slip and terrain curricula

**Learning quadrupedal locomotion over challenging terrain**, Joonho Lee, Jemin Hwangbo, Lorenz Wellhausen, Vladlen Koltun, Marco Hutter, **Science Robotics, 2020**, PEER-REVIEWED, cited-by 1107, `id: openalex:W3093922502`, `doi:10.1126/scirobotics.abc5986`. Privileged-learning + terrain curriculum trained entirely in sim, deployed zero-shot; no hardware-failure-seeded local curriculum. **No collision.**

**Learning to Walk in Minutes Using Massively Parallel Deep Reinforcement Learning**, Nikita Rudin, David Hoeller, Philipp Reist, Marco Hutter, **arXiv preprint, 2021** (also RA-L/CoRL), PREPRINT (per verified record; also has an ETH Zurich research-collection DOI `10.3929/ethz-b-000554960`), cited-by 101, `id: openalex:W4286963241`, `arXiv:2109.11978`.
Abstract (verified) references a "novel game-inspired curriculum" for massively parallel training. This curriculum promotes/demotes each parallel robot instance across a terrain difficulty bank based on per-robot success, i.e., training concentrates near each instance's own competence frontier. This is an **ANYmal-specific, success-boundary terrain curriculum**, structurally close to FBR's "train near the boundary" idea but entirely sim-internal (no hardware seeding), and the boundary is terrain-difficulty, not friction-around-an-observed-slip-event. **Partial collision on mechanism.**

**Rapid Locomotion via Reinforcement Learning**, Gabriel B. Margolis, Ge Yang, Kartik Paigwar, Tao Chen, Pulkit Agrawal, **RSS 2022**, PEER-REVIEWED, cited-by 120, `id: openalex:W4283787029`, `doi:10.15607/rss.2022.xviii.022`.
Abstract (verified) names "an adaptive curriculum on velocity commands" as a key component. This is the "grid/box-adaptive curriculum" flagged in the task: the command envelope (a box in velocity-command space) expands adaptively as the policy masters its current edge, structurally identical in spirit to ADR but specialized to locomotion command space rather than friction. **Major mechanism-level neighbor** in the legged-locomotion literature specifically: any reviewer with locomotion-RL background will immediately map FBR's "local neighborhood around the observed boundary" onto Margolis's box-adaptive curriculum and ask why friction-boundary curricula from real failures are different from command-boundary curricula from sim success rates. No hardware seeding in Margolis (curriculum driven by sim success rate), no single reconstructed failure state. **Strong partial collision, must be addressed head-on and used as a required baseline design (Section 5).**

**Robust Recovery Controller for a Quadrupedal Robot using Deep Reinforcement Learning**, Joonho Lee, Jemin Hwangbo, Marco Hutter, **arXiv preprint, 2019**, PREPRINT, cited-by 42, `id: openalex:W4288626282`, `arXiv:1901.07517`. Trains a fall/slip recovery policy broadly across randomly-initiated falls in sim; not seeded by any specific observed hardware failure, no local-neighborhood-of-a-boundary mechanism. **No collision**, but directly relevant as the "slip/recovery" locomotion baseline literature FBR sits adjacent to.

**Legged Robots that Keep on Learning: Fine-Tuning Locomotion Policies in the Real World**, Laura Smith, J. Chase Kew, Xue Bin Peng, Sehoon Ha, Jie Tan, Sergey Levine, **ICRA 2022**, PEER-REVIEWED, cited-by 96, `id: openalex:W3207033168`, `doi:10.1109/icra46639.2022.9812166`.
This is the closest **"deployment failure drives continued learning"** lineage item found: real hardware experience continuously fine-tunes the locomotion policy in the real world, with minimal-reset mechanisms. It is a **major structural neighbor** for the "real deployment experience improves robustness efficiently" research question, but it fine-tunes directly on **real-world rollouts** with an autonomous reset/safety scheme, it does not reconstruct a specific pre-failure state in **simulation**, does not perturb a local physical parameter neighborhood, and does not compare against broad domain randomization as a control arm (per the verified abstract's problem framing: real-world sample efficiency and safety, not sim-curriculum design). FBR's honest distinction: FBR keeps training entirely in simulation (cheap, safe, parallelizable) using a single real capsule as a seed, versus Smith et al.'s continued real-world training loop. This is a legitimate axis of novelty, provided FBR explicitly frames itself as "real-to-sim failure-seeded fine-tuning" and cites Smith et al. as the real-world-only alternative it is trying to avoid the cost of.

**Learning to Walk in the Real World with Minimal Human Effort**, Sehoon Ha, Peng Xu, Zhenyu Tan, Sergey Levine, Jie Tan, **arXiv preprint, 2020** (also CoRL 2020, `id: openalex:W3208422024`), PEER-REVIEWED (CoRL version confirmed) / PREPRINT (arXiv), cited-by 76 (arXiv) / 29 (CoRL), `id: openalex:W3007553593`, `arXiv:2002.08550`. Automates real-world RL for legged locomotion with learned safety/reset behaviors; same category as Smith et al., real-world-only training loop, not sim-reconstruction of a specific failure. **Partial collision**, same distinction applies.

### 3.5 Rare-event, counterfactual, and falsification-based testing/retraining

Importance-sampling toward rare failure scenarios for AV *testing/evaluation*, not policy retraining; the sampling-near-failure-region idea is a mechanism-level cousin of FBR's "sample the local neighborhood of a failure boundary," but the application is offline risk estimation, not on-policy fine-tuning, and scenarios are sim-generated, not a single logged real failure. **Partial collision on the sampling-near-failure-region idea.**

### 3.6 Real-to-sim failure reconstruction (closest overall lineage)

**Reconciling Reality through Simulation: A Real-to-Sim-to-Real Approach for Robust Manipulation (RialTo)**, Marcel Torne, Anthony Simeonov, Zechu Li, April Chan, Tao Chen, Manuel Gomez Gutierrez, Pulkit Agrawal, **arXiv preprint, 2024** (also RSS 2024, `id: openalex:W4402354100`, `doi:10.15607/rss.2024.xx.015`), PEER-REVIEWED (RSS version confirmed) / PREPRINT (arXiv), cited-by 5 (arXiv record), `id: openalex:W4392576663`, `arXiv:2403.03949`.
This is the closest **real-to-sim-to-real reconstruction-and-retrain** lineage item: a real environment/task is reconstructed into simulation (via scanning/digital-twin construction), domain randomization is applied around the reconstructed scene, and a policy is trained in sim and deployed back to the real robot. Structurally the nearest neighbor to "take something observed in the real world, rebuild it in sim, train there, redeploy." Key distinctions from FBR: (1) RialTo reconstructs a **scene/task**, not a **pre-failure dynamical state** triggered by a specific failure event; (2) it is a manipulation pipeline, not legged locomotion; (3) there is no notion of a "failure capsule" (state, command, policy hash, onset criterion) or of perturbing a local neighborhood around an observed **failure boundary** specifically, its domain randomization is applied broadly around the reconstructed scene, similar in spirit to standard DR rather than boundary-focused sampling. **Major collision on the real-to-sim-then-retrain paradigm; FBR must cite this explicitly as prior art for "real-world-observed-then-simulated-then-retrained" and differentiate on failure-boundary specificity and the locomotion/legged domain.**

**URDFormer: A Pipeline for Constructing Articulated Simulation Environments from Real-World Images**, Zoey Chen, Aaron Walsman, Marius Memmel, Kaichun Mo, Alex Chengyu Fang, Karthikeya Vemuri, Alan Xu, Dieter Fox, Abhishek Gupta, **arXiv preprint, 2024** (also RSS 2024), PEER-REVIEWED (RSS)/PREPRINT (arXiv), `id: openalex:W4398192197`, `arXiv:2405.11656`. **REFLECT: Summarizing Robot Experiences for Failure Explanation and Correction**, Zeyi Liu, Arpit Bahety, Shuran Song, **arXiv preprint, 2023**, PREPRINT, cited-by 18, `id: openalex:W4382603221`, `arXiv:2306.15724`. The "failure capsule" concept in FBR is conceptually adjacent to REFLECT's structured failure summary, but REFLECT does not fine-tune a policy or reset a simulator to a reconstructed pre-failure state; it produces language explanations and re-planning suggestions. **Partial collision on the "structured failure record" concept, no collision on the RL-retraining mechanism.**

**AHA: A Vision-Language-Model for Detecting and Reasoning Over Failures in Robotic Manipulation**, Jiafei Duan, Wilbert Pumacay, Nishanth Kumar, Yi Ru Wang, Shulin Tian, and additional authors, **arXiv preprint, 2024**, PREPRINT, cited-by 4, `id: openalex:W4403819024`, `arXiv:2410.00371`. ## 4. Novelty verdict

**Is FBR as stated already known? No single paper does exactly this, but every individual component is independently established, and several papers combine two or three of the components already.**

Known components, with the closest owner of each:
- **Reset-to-a-specific-state curriculum**: Florensa (reverse curriculum, 2017), Salimans & Chen (2018), Backplay (Resnick et al. 2018), Go-Explore (Ecoffet et al. 2019). All reset to a recorded/reconstructed state and locally push the curriculum outward from it.
- **Boundary-focused, auto-expanding domain randomization**: ADR (OpenAI 2019), Margolis's adaptive command-velocity box (RSS 2022), Rudin's game-inspired terrain curriculum (2021), Active Domain Randomization (Mehta et al. 2019). All train at the current edge of achievable difficulty in simulation, none seeded by a real hardware event.
- **Success/regret-boundary curricula over discrete or continuous environment parameters**: PLR/Robust PLR/ACCEL (regret-maximizing level or level-mutation selection), ALP-GMM (learning-progress-weighted continuous parameter sampling), GoalGAN (goals of intermediate difficulty).
- **Real rollouts reshaping a simulation parameter distribution**: SimOpt (Chebotar et al. 2019), BayesSim (Ramos et al. 2019), DROPO (Tiboni et al. 2022).
- **Real-to-sim reconstruction followed by simulated retraining and real redeployment**: RialTo (Torne et al. 2024), URDFormer (Chen et al. 2024) for the asset side.
- **Real deployment failures driving continued policy improvement**: Smith et al. (2022) and Ha et al. (2020), but entirely in the real world, no simulated reconstruction step.

**The smallest genuinely new combination, if any:** the specific triple of (a) a hardware-observed failure event, captured as a structured capsule (pre-failure state, command, policy hash, recent history, onset criterion), (b) reset to that reconstructed state **in simulation**, and (c) a local neighborhood **of a physically interpretable parameter (friction) plus small state perturbations** sampled around the *observed* boundary, fine-tuned under an **equal-compute budget comparison against broad DR**, does not appear as a single unit in any paper found. The closest single-paper approximations are: ADR (auto-expanding boundary, but sim-discovered, not hardware-seeded, and range-wide rather than local-neighborhood-around-one-event); Margolis's command-box curriculum (same critique, locomotion-specific); SimOpt/BayesSim/DROPO (real-seeded, but reshape a global distribution rather than replay one capsule and locally perturb around it); RialTo (real-to-sim-then-retrain, but scene reconstruction for manipulation, not a failure-triggered local friction neighborhood for locomotion); Salimans & Chen / reverse curriculum (reset-and-locally-explore mechanism, but from a demo/goal, not a failure, and no physics-parameter perturbation, only state perturbation).

**Strongest honest distinctions:**
- **vs. PLR/Robust PLR/ACCEL**: those operate over sim-generated discrete levels or level-mutations scored by a regret/learning-progress proxy; FBR's "hard case" is a single, real, physically observed failure, not a policy-internal regret signal. ACCEL's local-mutation-of-a-hard-level operator is the nearest mechanism, but it never touches hardware.
- **vs. broad domain randomization**: FBR's claim ("local, hardware-seeded curriculum beats broad DR at equal budget") is a reasonable, testable claim, but broad DR is a weak strawman on its own; ADR and Margolis already show that *any* boundary-focused (not necessarily hardware-seeded) sampling strategy beats uniform/broad DR. The interesting, defensible comparison is not "FBR vs. broad DR" but "FBR vs. a simulator-discovered boundary curriculum (ADR-style or PLR/ACCEL-style) at equal compute," because that is the comparison that isolates whether hardware-seeding specifically, rather than boundary-focusing generically, is what buys the efficiency gain.
- **vs. ADR**: ADR already trains at the edge of the current randomization range and expands it as competence grows. FBR must show its hardware-anchored local neighborhood is more sample/compute-efficient or more targeted at the *actual* failure mode than ADR's self-discovered boundary, which by construction may drift toward the wrong axis (e.g., might expand a mass or motor-strength range instead of friction, unless friction happens to be the binding constraint in ADR's own diagnostics).
- **vs. SimOpt**: SimOpt already closes a real-to-sim loop using real rollouts, but reshapes a global distribution to reduce sim-real discrepancy across many rollouts rather than exploiting one specific localized failure. FBR's argument must be about sample efficiency: one real failure event plus a local sim neighborhood should be cheaper and faster to exploit than SimOpt's iterative real-rollout-driven distribution fitting, at least for the specific, narrow, single-mode failure case (foot slip on a patch) rather than SimOpt's general sim-real gap.
- **vs. reverse curriculum (Florensa 2017)**: reverse curriculum resets near a known **success** (the goal) and expands outward to the initial state; FBR resets near a known **failure** and expands outward in a physical-parameter dimension (friction) plus state perturbation. This is a real and legitimate framing inversion (success-boundary vs. failure-boundary), but it means FBR should cite Florensa explicitly as the structural ancestor of "train in a local neighborhood of a boundary, receding/expanding over training," and should be honest that the polarity flip (goal-anchored vs. failure-anchored) is the novel bit, not the reset-and-locally-perturb mechanism itself.

**Should the claim be narrowed or pivoted?** Yes. As stated, "can a real quadruped failure define a local simulation curriculum near the observed failure boundary that improves robustness more efficiently than broad domain randomization" is too easily won against a weak baseline (broad DR) that the literature (ADR, Margolis, Rudin, Active DR) has already shown to be inefficient relative to *any* boundary-focused method, hardware-seeded or not. The most defensible framing is closer to: **"A hardware-observed failure event is a better (cheaper, more targeted, more sample-efficient) seed for a boundary-focused simulation curriculum than a simulator-discovered boundary (ADR-, PLR-, or ACCEL-style), at equal fine-tuning compute budget, for the specific failure mode actually observed on hardware."** This framing keeps the real contribution (hardware-in-the-loop failure specificity) as the object of study and requires the harder, more publishable baseline: not "vs. broad DR" but "vs. sim-only boundary-discovery."

## 5. Required baselines a CoRL/ICRA reviewer would demand

The proposed arms in the prompt are: A) no-repair, B) broad friction domain randomization, C) random-nominal-state replay control, D) FBR (hardware failure capsule, local friction + state neighborhood, PPO fine-tune). Given the literature above, a reviewer familiar with this space would flag the following gaps:

1. **A sim-only boundary-discovery arm (ADR-style or PLR/ACCEL-style), matched compute.** Without this, the paper cannot claim the benefit comes from *hardware-seeding* specifically rather than from *boundary-focusing* generically, which ADR/PLR/ACCEL/Margolis have already shown works. This is the single most important missing baseline.
2. **A SimOpt/BayesSim/DROPO-style arm**: use the same real rollout(s) that produced the failure capsule to fit/update a global (not local) sim parameter distribution, then fine-tune under the same compute budget, to isolate "local neighborhood around one event" from "global distribution shift from real data."
3. **A command/terrain-curriculum baseline in the style of Margolis (2022) or Rudin (2021)**, since these are the closest published locomotion-specific boundary-curricula and a reviewer in this subfield will ask for it directly.
4. **An online-adaptation baseline (RMA or UP-OSI style)** to show FBR's offline fine-tuning gain is not simply recoverable by cheaper test-time latent adaptation with no retraining at all. Right now the design has no arm that asks "what if we just deploy RMA/UP-OSI instead of retraining."
5. **A real-world continued-fine-tuning control in the spirit of Smith et al. (2022) or Ha et al. (2020)**, or at minimum an explicit discussion of why sim-only fine-tuning from one capsule is preferable to their real-world-loop approach, since that is the nearest "deployment failure improves the policy" lineage and omitting it invites a direct "why not just do what Smith et al. did" question.
6. **Ablation on capsule specificity**: an arm that resets to the correct pre-failure *state* but samples a broad (not local) friction range, and an arm that samples the correct local friction neighborhood but from a *generic* (not the specific observed) pre-failure state, to decompose how much of the gain comes from state-matching vs. parameter-localization. The current C) random-nominal-state-replay control only tests state-matching in one direction; it does not isolate the friction-neighborhood localization contribution on its own.
7. **A statement of the failure-detection/capsule-extraction assumption's generality**: none of the literature reviewed here addresses automatic extraction of a "failure capsule" (state, command, policy hash, history, onset criterion) from a real deployment run as a formal object; REFLECT is the closest (structured failure summarization) but produces language, not a replayable sim-reset object. A reviewer will ask how robust/automatic this extraction step is, and whether it generalizes beyond the default foot-slip failure mode.

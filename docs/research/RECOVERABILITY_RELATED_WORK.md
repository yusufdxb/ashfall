# Recoverability and replay-state selection: related work and novelty gate

Status: literature review done **before** any recoverability method was defined or any new
training was run, as the transition plan requires. Compiled 2026-09-22 by a literature-search
agent using OpenAlex, Crossref and the arXiv API (IDs verified there, quotes fetched), then
checked by hand. Venue claims the agent could not re-confirm are flagged in the last section.

## Ashfall's verdict (read this first)

1. **The general principle is not new.** "Train from states or tasks the current policy
   sometimes solves and sometimes fails; mastered and hopeless ones waste budget" is directly
   established: Reverse Curriculum Generation (Florensa et al. 2017, arXiv:1707.05300,
   "good starts" with intermediate success probability), Wohlke et al. 2020 (AAMAS,
   performance-based start-state curricula), ProCuRL (Tzannetos et al., arXiv:2304.12877,
   "neither too hard nor too easy"), and above all **Sampling For Learnability** (Rutherford et
   al., NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2408.15099): it scores levels by rollout-estimated learnability
   `p(1-p)` and trains on the most learnable ones mixed with random ones. DAPO (2025) applies
   the same filter to LLM RL. Ashfall must cite these as the origin of the idea and must not
   present "intermediate recoverability is best" as its contribution.
2. **"Recoverability-Guided Replay" is not a new algorithm.** Rollout-estimated `R`, a band,
   a mix with broad randomization, recomputation as the policy improves and a frontier that
   advances along a trajectory are, respectively, SFL / RCG, SFL, RCG, and Salimans and Chen
   2018 / Backplay / ADR. At most it is SFL-style selection applied to states on a failure
   trajectory. If it is ever built it is named as that, not as a method contribution.
3. **What was not found in prior work** (and so is the only claim Ashfall may make if the data
   support it):
   - (a) the **provenance-null**: for repairing an already-trained policy, states from a real
     failure, a simulator-found failure and a successful trajectory are interchangeable once
     matched (already measured in the precursor sweep; the retrospective tests whether matching
     on `R` is enough);
   - (b) a head-to-head of **recoverability against time-to-failure, distance to the hazard and
     provenance** as predictors of fixed-budget **repair** value of an already-trained policy.
   Both are empirical, narrow and toy-scale unless reproduced in Isaac Lab.
4. **Stop-rule decision.** The user's stop condition was prior work that *directly* establishes
   "intermediate recoverability predicts the highest policy-repair value under matched
   budgets". No paper found does this for *repair of a trained policy* or compares against
   time-to-failure or provenance, so the retrospective analysis (which tests exactly (b)) goes
   ahead. But the method claim is dead on novelty grounds, which raises the bar for
   Phase 7 onward: a new confirmatory study is only worth running if it tests (a) or (b), not
   "a new curriculum beats DR".

Additional gap noted by hand: quadruped fall-recovery work (for example FR-Net,
arXiv:2509.11504; Curricular Hindsight RL, Sci. Rep. 2024) learns recovery *controllers*; none
found selects repair-training states by measured recoverability. The fall-prediction
literature was not searched exhaustively (the agent's two query phrasings returned nothing
relevant), so absence there is not established.

The proposed Ashfall claim has two parts: (A) an empirical claim that intermediate recoverability R_pi(s) start states maximize *policy-repair* value under a *fixed training budget* for an *already-trained* policy, with R_pi(s) beating time-to-failure and trajectory-provenance as a predictor; (B) a method, "Recoverability-Guided Replay," that estimates R by branched rollouts, keeps an intermediate band, retrains, and re-estimates R as the policy improves to advance a frontier.

## Notes with IDs and quotes

- Florensa et al., "Reverse Curriculum Generation for Reinforcement Learning," arXiv:1707.05300 (2017), PEER-REVIEWED via CoRL 2017 proceedings (OpenAlex lists it as arXiv preprint id but the venue is CoRL; treat as PEER-REVIEWED conference paper, cited-by 140 in OpenAlex). Quote: "The robot is trained in reverse, gradually learning to reach the goal from a set of start states increasingly far from the goal. Our method automatically generates a curriculum of start states that adapts to the agent's performance." This is the origin of the "keep states with intermediate success probability" idea the brief asks about, but it is a from-scratch training method, not a repair-of-an-already-trained-policy method, and it never compares real-failure states to sim-found or successful-trajectory states.

- Wohlke, Schmitt, van Hoof, "A Performance-Based Start State Curriculum Framework for Reinforcement Learning," AAMAS 2020, DOI 10.65109/llzi8531 (Crossref-confirmed; original venue IFAAMAS/ACM, PDF at ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2020/pdfs/p1503.pdf, fetched and converted to text directly). PEER-REVIEWED. Quote (from the fetched PDF): "Closest to our work is the 'Reverse Curriculum' generation approach [9]. New start states are found nearby 'good states' that have an intermediate probability of reaching the goal." This paper explicitly names "intermediate probability of reaching the goal" as the key mechanism and generalizes it into a "performance-based start state curriculum" framework, still for training from scratch, still without a repair-of-pretrained-policy framing, without any failure-log / provenance comparison, and without a matched-budget high/med/low sweep specifically on an already-trained policy's own recoverability collapse point.

- Ivanovic et al., BaRC, arXiv:1806.06161, ICRA 2019 (DOI 10.1109/icra.2019.8794206, PEER-REVIEWED). Quote: "Our Backward Reachability Curriculum (BaRC) begins policy training from states that require a small number of actions to accomplish the task, and expands the initial state distribution backwards in a dynamically-consistent manner once the policy optimization algorithm demonstrates sufficient performance." Model-based reachability expansion, not a rollout-estimated recoverability measure, not repair, no provenance comparison.

- Resnick et al., "Backplay," arXiv:1807.06919 (2018), PREPRINT (no peer-reviewed venue found in OpenAlex/Crossref). Quote: "we start the agent near the end of the demonstration and move the starting point backwards during the course of training until we reach the initial state." Schedule-based frontier advance from a single demo, not recoverability-estimated, not repair of a trained-then-failing policy, no provenance comparison (only one demo is used, by definition).

- Salimans & Chen, "Learning Montezuma's Revenge from a Single Demonstration," arXiv:1812.03381 (2018), PREPRINT. Quote: "Our agent is trained using off-the-shelf reinforcement learning, but starts every episode by resetting to a state from a demonstration. By starting from such demonstration states, the agent requires much less exploration." Advances the reset point backward along the demo as success rate improves, closely resembling the "advance the frontier" half of Recoverability-Guided Replay, but built on one successful demo, no notion of repairing an already-trained mastered-then-failing policy, no provenance comparison (real failure vs sim vs success), no explicit R_pi(s) vs time-to-failure regression.

- Tao et al., "Reverse Forward Curriculum Learning for Extreme Sample and Demonstration Efficiency in Reinforcement Learning," arXiv:2405.03379, ICLR 2024 (PEER-REVIEWED, per the task brief; OpenAlex classifies the record as a preprint type but ICLR is the actual peer-reviewed venue). Quote: "Our approach consists of a reverse curriculum followed by a forward curriculum... The result of our reverse curriculum is an initial policy that performs well on a narrow initial state distribution and helps overcome difficult exploration problems. A forward curriculum is then used to accelerate the training of the initial policy to perform well on the full initial state distribution." This is a from-demonstration bootstrapping method (build a policy, don't repair one that already works elsewhere and is failing near a specific mode), and per WebFetch summary of the abstract, it does not mention success-probability-based reset selection or fixed-budget repair.

- Tzannetos et al., "Proximal Curriculum for Reinforcement Learning Agents" (ProCuRL), arXiv:2304.12877 (2023). PREPRINT at this ID; the 2024 IJCAI paper "Proximal Curriculum with Task Correlations for Deep Reinforcement Learning" (DOI 10.24963/ijcai.2024/556, arXiv:2405.02481) is the PEER-REVIEWED follow-up. Quote: "ProCuRL captures the intuition that learning progress is maximized when picking tasks that are neither too hard nor too easy for the learner." This is the most direct theoretical statement of "intermediate difficulty maximizes learning progress," derived from the Zone of Proximal Development, applied to task/context selection generally, not to R_pi(s) computed by branched rollouts from a trained policy's own failure trajectory, not to repair under a fixed budget, not to provenance comparisons.

- Rutherford et al., "No Regrets: Investigating and Improving Regret Approximations for Curriculum Discovery," arXiv:2408.15099 (2024). PREPRINT (no peer-reviewed venue confirmed via OpenAlex/Crossref in this session; treat as PREPRINT/GRAY pending confirmation). Quote: "Surprisingly, despite methods aiming to maximise regret in theory, the practical approximations do not correlate with regret but with success rate. As a result, a significant portion of an agent's experience comes from environments it has already mastered, offering little to no contribution... current methods fail to predict intuitive measures of 'learnability.' Specifically, they are unable to consistently identify those scenarios that the agent can sometimes solve, but not always. Based on our analysis, we develop a method that directly trains on scenarios with high learnability." This is the closest generic statement to the Ashfall claim: intermediate/variable success-rate scenarios (learnability, effectively p(1-p)) beat both "mastered" and (implicitly) unsolved scenarios for training value, and the paper explicitly argues against training on already-mastered states. But: it is about environment/level selection in UED for training, not about repairing an already-deployed, previously-competent-then-failing policy under a matched budget; it does not compare to time-to-failure as a competing predictor; it has no notion of trajectory provenance (real failure log vs sim-found vs successful trajectory).

- Yu et al., "DAPO: An Open-Source LLM Reinforcement Learning System at Scale," arXiv:2503.14476 / DOI 10.52202/085713-3775 (2025), PEER-REVIEWED (per Crossref record, conference-paper). Quote: "we introduce four key techniques... Dynamic Sampling" filters training prompts, dropping those where the group's accuracy is 0 or 1 (i.e., always right or always wrong), and keeping intermediate-difficulty prompts. This corroborates that the p(1-p)/"drop the extremes" heuristic is well established engineering practice in a completely different domain (LLM RL), reinforcing that the *general* intermediate-difficulty-is-best principle is not novel; it says nothing about robots, recoverability, or repair.

- Heim, Rohr, et al., "A Learnable Safety Measure," arXiv:1910.02835 (2019), CoRL 2019 PEER-REVIEWED (per CoRL proceedings; not independently re-confirmed via Crossref this session, flagged). Quote: "We present a safety measure which implicitly captures how the system dynamics relate to a set of failure states... we show a model-free approach to learn this measure by active sampling using Gaussian processes." A genuine learned recoverability/viability-style measure for failure avoidance during exploration, not used to select replay/reset states for repairing a previously-trained, now-failing policy, and not compared to time-to-failure or provenance.

- Grinsztajn et al., "There Is No Turning Back: A Self-Supervised Approach for Reversibility-Aware Reinforcement Learning," arXiv:2106.04480 (2021), PREPRINT (NeurIPS 2021 is the actual peer-reviewed venue per public record, not independently re-confirmed via Crossref this session). Quote: "we propose to learn to distinguish reversible from irreversible actions... pairs of events that are always observed in the same order are likely to be separated by an irreversible sequence of actions." This is a reversibility (can-I-undo-this-action) estimate, not a recoverability-to-success estimate, and it is used for exploration/control biasing, not for choosing training reset states.

- Thananjeyan et al., "Recovery RL: Safe Reinforcement Learning With Learned Recovery Zones," IEEE RA-L 2021, DOI 10.1109/lra.2021.3070252, PEER-REVIEWED. - Hsu, Fisac et al., "DeepReach: A Deep Learning Approach to High-Dimensional Reachability," ICRA 2021, DOI 10.1109/icra48506.2021.9561949, PEER-REVIEWED. - Jiang et al., "Prioritized Level Replay," arXiv:2010.03934 (2020), ICML 2021 PEER-REVIEWED (per public record; not independently re-confirmed via Crossref this session). - Jiang et al., "ACCEL: Evolving Curricula with Regret-Based Environment Design," arXiv:2203.01302 (2022), NeurIPS 2022 PEER-REVIEWED (per public record). - Ecoffet et al., "Go-Explore," arXiv:1901.10995 (2019), Nature 2021 (published version) PEER-REVIEWED. Return-to-a-promising-archived-state-then-explore mechanic; no explicit success-probability model, no repair-of-trained-policy framing, no provenance comparison.

## Verdict

**(1) Is "intermediate success-probability start states give the most learning value under a matched budget" ALREADY DIRECTLY ESTABLISHED?**
The *general principle* (intermediate difficulty/success-rate maximizes learning value, extremes are wasted compute) is well established and appears repeatedly and independently: Florensa et al. 2017 (RCG, "intermediate probability of reaching the goal"), Wohlke et al. 2020 (names this explicitly as the mechanism behind RCG and generalizes it), Tzannetos et al. 2023/2024 (ProCuRL, ZPD theory), Rutherford et al. 2024 (learnability = "can sometimes solve, but not always," explicitly rejects training on mastered states), and DAPO 2025 (drops accuracy-0 and accuracy-1 prompts). So the qualitative claim "intermediate is best, mastered and unsolvable are wasted" is NOT novel in general. What is NOT directly established by any of these: none of them (a) operate on an already-trained, previously-competent, now-failing policy being repaired, (b) hold a training budget fixed and measure repair value specifically, or (c) compare R_pi(s) against time-to-failure or trajectory provenance as competing predictors.

**(2) Is "Recoverability-Guided Replay" already an existing method under another name?**
Its core mechanics exist piecemeal and overlap heavily: rollout-estimated success probability from a candidate state (RCG, Wohlke et al.), keeping/training on an intermediate band (RCG, ProCuRL, Rutherford et al., DAPO), and advancing a frontier as the policy improves (Salimans & Chen, Backplay, ACCEL, ADR). No single verified paper combines all of: (i) estimating R by branched continuation rollouts from states drawn from an actual failure trajectory of an already-trained policy, (ii) restricting to a band, (iii) recomputing R as the policy improves, and (iv) advancing the frontier toward the failure, applied to *repair* rather than initial training. It is best described as a recombination of RCG-style band selection with Backplay/ADR-style frontier advance, retargeted at post-deployment repair.

**(3) Residual novelty.** Narrow and specific, not the general "intermediate is best" idea: (a) the provenance-null finding, that for repair, reset states from a real failure log, a simulator-found failure, and a successful trajectory at matched time/place are interchangeable, has no counterpart found in this search, none of the surveyed papers test provenance as a variable; (b) the explicit head-to-head of R_pi(s) versus time-to-failure as competing predictors of repair value, under a matched budget, on an already-trained (not from-scratch) policy, is also not found; (c) reframing all of this as *repair* of a fielded, previously-mastered-then-failing policy (versus curriculum for training from scratch) is a genuine framing delta versus every close paper above, though it is a framing/application delta, not a new algorithmic primitive. Recommend the paper lead with (a) and (b) as the falsifiable, testable claims, and explicitly cite Florensa 2017, Wohlke 2020, Tzannetos 2023/2024, and Rutherford 2024 as prior art for the general intermediate-difficulty principle so a reviewer cannot flag it as the novelty.
